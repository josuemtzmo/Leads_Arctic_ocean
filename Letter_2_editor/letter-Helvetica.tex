Dear Editor,

On behalf of my coauthors, I am pleased to submit our manuscript ``{\bf Is the surface forcing in sea ice leads transferred to the Arctic ocean interior?}" for consideration as an article in \textit{Nature Geoscience}. 

The sea-ice acts as a natural insulator between the atmosphere and the ocean by limiting heat loss and minimizing evaporation. Leads are ubiquitous across the polar oceans and they are windows between the ocean and the atmosphere that can allow large flux exchanges. Several studies have provided evidence that leads can locally impact the ocean surface and likely set up locally properties of the Arctic Ocean mixed layer. Nonetheless, the integrated impact leads have in the large scale buoyancy forcing at the ocean surface across the Arctic basin has not been quantified yet. \textbf{Here, we present the first estimate of the contribution of the buoyancy forcing and water mass transformation within leads in the Arctic Ocean.} Our results also show that leads have larger buoyancy fluxes in the ice pack and the MIZ compared to the same regions excluding the leads. Despite these larger fluxes, water mass transformation in the leads is proportional to their area coverage. 


Our findings motivate the need to better represent eddy-sea ice interactions in numerical models, since in climate models, the heterogeneity of sea ice caused by eddies is missing. Our findings have major implications, since the effect of eddies during ice growth has been ignored and is required to better understand the eddy-atmosphere coupling, in order to improve our capacity to better predict the current and future evolution of Arctic sea ice.

\vspace{-2em}
\subsection*{Summary of scientific contribution}
\vspace{-1em}

The study investigates the role of leads in the Arctic Ocean, focusing on their impact on surface buoyancy forcing and water mass transformation. Using a high-resolution hindcast model (SEDNA), the authors quantify the contribution of leads to these processes, contrasting their effects with those of the surrounding ice-covered regions. Key findings include the estimation that leads cover approximately 20\% of the sea-ice covered Arctic and can contribute up to 20\% more buoyancy flux compared to areas outside leads in the ice pack. However, despite their significant buoyancy fluxes, leads only account for around 20\% of the total surface water mass transformation, indicating a smaller contribution than previously assumed. The study highlights that while leads induce localized changes in surface water density, their frequency, extent, and duration are not sufficient to significantly alter water masses across the Arctic Ocean. Moreover, the analysis suggests that leads have a limited imprint on ocean surface and interior properties, challenging previous hypotheses regarding their impact on Arctic Ocean dynamics. The study also discusses potential future impacts of leads on Arctic climate and emphasizes the importance of dedicated research to understand their evolving role in the context of a changing Arctic environment.

%Oceanic eddies are known to impact sea ice in the polar regions, particularly in the marginal ice zone (the zone of transition between the ocean and the pack ice). Eddies regulate the lateral redistribution of heat, salt and momentum. Yet up until now, the response of sea ice growing in a eddy rich ocean has remained unexplored in detail. Here we use two numerical models, a ultra high-resolution hindcast that captures the complexity of growth of sea ice under oceanic and atmospheric forcing, and a simplified process model that only retains the first order processes of interest (mesoscale eddies). Our results show that eddies introduce heterogeneity in the sea ice, with the potential to make sea ice more brittle.

\vspace{-2em}
\subsection*{General relevance}
\vspace{-1em}

Understanding the role of leads in the Arctic Ocean is crucial for accurately modeling and predicting Arctic climate dynamics. This study provides valuable insights into the contribution of leads to surface buoyancy forcing and water mass transformation, challenging previous assumptions about their significance. By utilizing a high-resolution hindcast model, the authors offer a comprehensive analysis of leads' impact on Arctic Ocean processes. The findings have implications for climate modeling efforts aimed at improving the representation of Arctic Ocean dynamics and addressing existing challenges in simulating stratification and water masses. Additionally, the study underscores the need for further research to assess the evolving role of leads in the context of ongoing Arctic climate change. Overall, this research contributes to advancing our understanding of Arctic Ocean dynamics and informs future studies on the role of leads in shaping Arctic climate.


% Eddies are ubiquitous features of the sea ice free and ice covered oceans. Eddies in the Arctic have been observed in satellite images where tracers such as temperature, salinity, and sea ice are advected within the swirly structures. In this paper we present, for the first time, a detailed study of the sea ice forming above an eddy reach ocean using a simplified model that only includes the interactions between the ocean and the ice. Our analysis shows a clear response of the sea ice formation to the presence of oceanic eddies. The heterogeneity of sea ice increases with the intensity of the eddies, furthermore more heterogeneity in the ice has major implications on how the ice could response to winds and storms. Our project focuses on a scale of interaction between the ocean and sea ice that has been overlooked in climate models. Better representation of the interactions between mesoscale eddies and the sea ice is crucial to better understand how the sea ice in the Arctic works, to better predict its evolution and to reduce the uncertainty in the prediction of global warming.
