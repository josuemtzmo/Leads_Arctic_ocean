\begin{abstract}
    The Arctic sea-ice, in particular the ice-pack, acts as an insulator between the atmosphere and the ocean below. Linear kinematic features (LKF), or leads, common in Arctic sea ice serves as openings that allow exchanges between ocean and atmosphere, for example: heat, salt, moisture, and gases. Therefore, leads can influence heat exchanges, ice melt or growth, ocean dynamics, and the atmospheric boundary layer. The scale of leads varies from a few meters to kilometers in width, and from few kilometers up to hundreds of kilometers in length. Their formation is driven by the winds and ocean currents. Existing literature has suggested that leads play a significant role in the heat budget of the surface of the ocean and likely impact water mass transformation within the Arctic. However, due to the lack of observations and high resolution models resolving LKFs, an estimate of the magnitude of the impact in the ocean has been elusive.  Here, we explore the magnitude and impact in the ocean surface caused by the LKFs using SEDNA, a high resolution pan-Arctic hindcast (1/60$^\circ$; NEMO + SI3 configuration). We show that despite the LKF only account for a small area of the sea-ice cover (<5\%), they contribute up to 20-30\% more buoyancy than the ice-pack at the surface. Furthermore, of the total surface water mass transformation in the Arctic ice cover, LKFs explain approximately 10\% of it.  LKFs make a significant contribution despite their small area coverage; however, the presented estimate shows a large local impact but to a lesser degree in the Arctic Ocean than previously hypothesized.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% Paragraph topic: Discuss Sea ice, insulator properties, and LKF

The Arctic sea-ice plays a crucial role in regulating Earth's climate by serving as a natural insulator between the atmosphere and the ocean below \citep{Wettlaufer_insulator_1997, Untersteiner_ice_insulator_1961}. This insulator property acts as a thermal barrier, limiting heat loss from the ocean to the atmosphere and minimizing evaporation, thereby it maintains the stability of the polar environment \citep{Ruffieux_pack_lead_1995}. An ubiquitous feature of the Arctic sea ice is the formation and persistence of linear kinematic features (LKF), commonly known as leads. These features are elongated openings of the sea ice cover that expose the ocean surface to the atmosphere \citep{Lupkes_leads_2008}. 

% Paragraph topic: LKF scales, LKF formation mechanisms + climatology.

Leads vary in scale from a few meters to kilometers in width, and span distances of a few kilometers up to hundreds of kilometers \citep{Tschudi_obs_leads_1998}. Their formation mechanisms are diverse, ranging from mechanical to thermodynamic processes \citep{Rampal_nextsim_2016, Hutchings_model_lkf_2005}. Mechanically, sea ice deformation and fracturing are caused by winds and ocean currents \citep{Richter_pack_stress_2002}. Winds are thought to be the main forcing of the sea ice deformation, where persistent winds stress result in sea ice fracturing, and eventually leads \citep{Linow_lkf_2017, Hutter_scaling_lkf_2018}. Although ocean currents have a smaller contribution to the sea ice deformation compared to the wind forcing, ocean drag at the bottom of the ice acts to balance the wind-induced sea ice velocities \citep{Weiss_scale_2004}. Additionally, observations show that ocean currents may play a bigger role in dictating the fracturing patterns of sea ice in regions with strong currents and bath features \citep{Willmes_leads_currents_2023}. Alongside these mechanical forces, thermodynamic processes such as a rapid variation of the air or ocean temperature can result in the fracturing of sea ice \citep{Lewis_thermomechanics_1998}. Once a lead is formed, warm air temperatures can instigate localized melting maintaining the lead open. Conversely, during colder periods, rapid freezing facilitate the close a lead. The intricate interplay between these thermodynamic processes and dynamic forces dictates the evolution and persistence of leads.

% \textbf{Describe believed importance in the fluxes and likely impact to WMT.}
Leads are crucial since they act as gateways between the atmosphere and the ocean, exchanging heat, moisture, and gases \citep{Michaelis_lead_2022, Andreas_vapor_2002}. Depending on the season and the atmospheric forcing, this direct contact between the ocean water and atmosphere can result in locally intensified heat fluxes at the ocean-atmosphere interphase \citep{Marcq_flux_lead_2012,Maykut_heat_1986}, localized ice melt or growth, and modify the ocean dynamics \citep{Bourgault_flux_lkf_2020}. For example, in winter heat exchange from the ocean to the cold atmosphere, in addition to ice freezing within the leads, result in local convective processes in both the ocean and atmosphere \citep{Lupkes_leads_2008,McPhee_LKF_2005}. A change in the surface temperature and salinity due to the heat and salt flux at the ocean-ice -atmosphere interfaces transform locally the surface water masses \citep{Walin_WMT_1982}. Leads have a undeniable local impact in the ocean and it has been hypothesized that leads may play a significant role in the Arctic by modulating the stratification and water mass transformation \citep{}. However, the magnitude and influence of the LKFs compared to the surrounding ice pack in the Arctic basin is yet to be quantified.

\section{Methods}
\subsection*{Model description}


The study employs the NEMO numerical platform (release 4.0.5) to establish a 1/60$^\circ$ pan-Arctic ocean-sea ice configuration named SEDNA. The ocean core (NEMO-OCE) solves the primitive equations (PE) using finite differences on an Arakawa C-grid mesh, while the ocean is coupled to the SI3 sea-ice component (NEMO-SI3). The pan-Arctic domain covers the Bering Strait on the Pacific side and extends southward to 56$^\circ$N in the Subpolar North Atlantic gyre and to the Baltic Sea entrance. The ocean model incorporates a linear free surface, utilizes a 3rd order flux form scheme for momentum advection, and employs a 4th order Flux Corrected Transport (FCT) for tracer advection. Additionally, the sea-ice representation includes a 5-category model with the Elasto-Visco-Plastic (EVP) rheology activated and a land-fast capability to better simulate sea-ice behavior in specific areas.

The NCAR bulk formula from Large \& Yeager (2009) to calculate air-sea fluxes and uses hourly ERA5 atmospheric data for near-surface variables. The three lateral boundaries are constrained with daily GLORYS12V1 Re-analysis data, and monthly freshwater fluxes from river discharges are combined with Greenland land ice melt data to supply SEDNA. The simulation extends over seven years (2009 to 2015) and starts from rest, utilizing initial conditions based on the World Ocean Atlas 2009 temperature/salinity and mean January 2009 ice state from PIOMAS re-analysis. Preliminary assessment against available observations and re-analyses data shows that the simulation reasonably captures key ocean and sea-ice characteristics.

\subsection*{LKF identification}

The LKF from SEDNA were identified by using the algorithm proposed by \citet{Hutter_identification_2019}. This method uses the total deformation rate defined as: 
\begin{equation}
    T_d = \left[\left(\underbrace{\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} +\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}}_{Divergence}\right)^2 + \underbrace{\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial v}{\partial y}\right)^2+ \left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\right)^2}_{Shear}\right]^{1/2},
\end{equation}
where $u$ and $v$ are the sea ice velocities. The deformation rate varies spatially depending on the background deformation and the sea ice properties \citep{}, where the largest deformation rates occur along the boundaries of ice flows where the LKF are found. Therefore, to identify the LKFs it is required to identify the local maxima using edge detection (DoG filter) in the deformation rate field. After the edge detection, using binary map we obtain a mask with the original width of identified LKF. This mask is then used to compute the statistics presented in this study. After this step, \citet{Hutter_identification_2019} reduce the LKFs width to a single pixel to then track the LKF over time. Since our study focus on the ocean-atmosphere interactions, we only use the identification algorithm to produce a mask of LKFs during 2014 for the Arctic, where we retain the original width computed from the deformation rate. This mask is then used to quantify and contrast the impact of LKFs compared to the ice-pack.
